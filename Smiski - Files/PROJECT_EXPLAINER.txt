0. THE 30-SECOND VERSION
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Smiskis are tiny glow-in-the-dark figures sold in blind boxes (you do
not know which figure you get). I built a data science project that
asks three questions:

  1. Why do some figures resell for much more than the box price?
  2. Is interest in Smiskis growing, and when did it take off?
  3. What do collectors talk about online, and is their mood an early
     warning for demand?

Each question became one model (one Jupyter notebook). Then I built a
website with a "virtual blind box": pick a series, pick a box, open it,
and you get a random figure with a model-estimated resale value.

>>> IMPORTANT, SAY THIS OUT LOUD: part of the data is REAL and part is
>>> SIMULATED. Section 1 explains exactly which. Do not skip it.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHAT IS REAL AND WHAT IS SIMULATED (the honest scorecard)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
REAL (collected from the web):
  - The 18 Smiski blind-box series and their 108 named figures, with
    the official one-line descriptions.             -> smiski.com
  - Weekly search interest for "smiski", Dec 2017 - Oct 2026.
                                                    -> Google Trends
  - Product launches, store openings and announcements (36 news
    posts plus 18 series launches).                 -> smiski.com news
  - The official product photos used on the website (shown locally
    only; see "Images" in section 7).               -> smiski.com

SIMULATED (generated by my own code, because no accessible source):
  - Resale prices and premiums. Marketplaces (eBay etc.) do not allow
    simple scraping, so I generated realistic-looking prices using
    rules I wrote (rare figures cost more, early weeks after launch
    cost more, etc.).
  - Reddit posts. The Reddit API needs a login key I do not have yet,
    so I generated 3,600 posts from templates.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR EACH MODEL:
  Model 2 (demand forecast)  -> runs 100% on REAL data.
  Model 1 (resale premium)   -> real figures/series, SIMULATED prices.
  Model 3 (Reddit)           -> SIMULATED posts.
  Models 1 and 3 prove the METHOD works end to end. Their numbers are
  NOT facts about real Smiski buyers. The website shows a banner that
  says this, and every notebook prints the same warning.

ASSUMPTIONS I HAD TO MAKE (the official site does not publish these):
  - Chance of pulling the secret figure = 1 in 72 (a number collectors
    quote; the maker does not publish odds). The 6 regular figures
    share the rest equally.
  - Retail price = $12.99 for every series (the site shows no prices).
  - Each series has one secret figure, but the site never names it,
    so it appears as "Secret".
  - Release dates: exact for newer series (taken from news posts),
    approximate for older ones (taken from when the series page's
    pictures were uploaded). Marked as such in the data.
  - Hippers is left out: it is an attachable-figure product with no
    secret, not a normal blind-box series.

2. HOW THE PROJECT WAS BUILT - STEP BY STEP
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Step 1  Set up Python + Jupyter (pandas, scikit-learn, LightGBM,
        XGBoost, SHAP, statsmodels, Prophet, ruptures, BERTopic).
Step 2  Collect real data with small "scrapers" that are polite:
        they obey the site's robots.txt, wait 2 seconds between
        requests, and cache what they download.
          - smiski_catalog.py : reads each series page -> figure names
          - smiski_news.py    : reads news posts -> launches, stores
          - google_trends.py  : downloads search interest
          - smiski_images.py  : downloads figure photos (local only)
Step 3  Assemble clean tables (series, figures, trends, events,
        resale, reddit). Where a real source is missing, a simulator
        fills in the gap and the table is labelled "simulated".
Step 4  Run three notebooks, one per model, each ending with a
        plain-English business takeaway.
Step 5  Export the results as small data files and build the website
        (plain HTML/CSS/JavaScript, no server needed).
Step 6  Test it: 10 automated tests, plus a check that the website
        runs without errors.

Network note: this computer's security sandbox blocks most websites.
I only opened the specific sites needed (smiski.com, Google Trends,
Reddit) after getting approval. Wikipedia and eBay were not used.

3. MODEL 1 - RESALE PREMIUM  (notebook 01_resale_premium)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
IN SIMPLE TERMS
  "Premium" = how much MORE than the shelf price a figure sells for
  on the resale market. Example: $12.99 box resells for $26 -> about
  +100% premium. The model learns which traits push the premium up.

INPUTS (what the model looks at for each figure)
  series theme, rarity (regular vs secret), retail price, season of
  release, months since launch, whether it was a collab, and search
  interest around launch (real Google Trends).

SOURCES
  Series/figures/launch dates: REAL (smiski.com).
  Search interest at launch:   REAL (Google Trends).
  Resale prices:               SIMULATED (see Section 1).

HOW IT WAS RUN
  - Models compared: a plain average (baseline), "average per rarity"
    (a smarter baseline), linear regression, LightGBM, XGBoost.
  - TESTING TRICK: figures in the same series look alike, so a random
    train/test split cheats (the model "recognises" the series). I
    held out WHOLE series instead (leave-one-series-out), and also
    trained on older series and tested on the 3 newest.
  - SHAP: a tool that shows which inputs the model leans on.
  - Prediction ranges: built from the model's past errors.

RESULTS (on simulated prices - describe the simulation, not reality)
  - Typical error (average miss, in percentage points of premium):
      plain average 21.8 | rarity average 12.4 | linear 11.7
      LightGBM 12.0      | XGBoost 12.0
  - The fancy models beat the "rarity average" baseline by only about
    7-8%, and the uncertainty range includes zero. Plain English:
    "knowing whether a figure is rare gets you most of the way;
    the extra models add little reliable accuracy."
  - SHAP: rarity dominates (about 18.6 points), far ahead of theme
    (4.4), months since launch (3.2), season (1.4), launch hype (1.3).
    Retail price and collab show 0 because I used one price and found
    no collab series.
  - Cheating check: a random split would have scored R-squared 0.87;
    the honest whole-series test scores 0.74. That gap is leakage.
  - Prediction ranges meant to cover 80% of cases only covered 57% on
    the newest series, so treat ranges as optimistic.

BUSINESS TAKEAWAY (as the notebook states it)
  Rarity matters most; timing and theme matter a little; a simple
  model is about as good as a complex one with this little data
  (only 18 series = 18 truly independent examples).

4. MODEL 2 - DEMAND FORECAST + CHANGEPOINTS  (02_demand_forecast)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
IN SIMPLE TERMS
  Google Trends gives a 0-100 "how much are people searching for
  Smiski" score each week. I (a) tried to forecast it and (b) looked
  for the moments the trend suddenly changed gear, then checked
  whether those moments line up with real product launches.

SOURCES  (100% REAL)
  Google Trends, worldwide, weekly, "smiski" (459 weeks).
  Google only gives weekly data for ~5-year windows, so I downloaded
  two overlapping windows and stitched them together.
  Events: smiski.com news + series launch dates.

THE STORY THE REAL DATA TELLS
  Average search score by year: under 1 until 2021, 1.5 in 2022,
  9 in 2023, 44 in 2024, 72 in 2025, 64 in 2026. So the craze took
  off in 2023 and peaked in 2025. Searches also bump up each
  December (about +28% above the trend).

HOW IT WAS RUN
  - Six forecasters: "same as last week" (naive), "same as this
    week last year", SARIMAX, Prophet, Prophet + launch calendar,
    and a LightGBM model using past values.
  - FAIR TEST: forecast the final 52 weeks the models never saw.
    The winner was picked from earlier "practice" forecasts, not from
    the final exam.
  - CHANGEPOINTS: an algorithm (ruptures / PELT) scans the line for
    sudden level shifts, after removing the December cycle.

RESULTS
  - Average miss on the final year (lower is better):
      naive 9.6 | seasonal naive 9.8 | SARIMAX 11.8 | LightGBM 12.1
      Prophet ~106 (it extrapolated growth and badly overshot).
  - NOTHING BEAT "same as last week". Search interest peaked in 2025
    and then eased, which none of the models saw coming.
  - Six changepoints found; four land within 8 weeks of a real event:
      Dec 2022 (about 5 weeks from a product launch)
      May 2023 Exercising Series launch (+348% jump)
      Jan 2024 Moving Series launch
      Sep 2024 HIPPERS release (+86% jump)
    Two others (Oct 2021, Jul 2022) sit about 16-17 weeks from the
    Work/Dressing launches, so I call those unmatched.
  - Forecast range: the model's own range was too wide (covered
    100%); a range built from past errors was too narrow (about 2%).
    The website draws the wider one and says so.

BUSINESS TAKEAWAY
  Plan for launches and December, but do not trust a fancy forecast
  over "same as last week" yet. Changepoints near launches are
  suggestive, not proof (many things happen at once).

5. MODEL 3 - WHAT COLLECTORS TALK ABOUT  (03_reddit_nlp)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
IN SIMPLE TERMS
  Read lots of fan posts, automatically group them into topics
  (resale complaints, display ideas, restock frustration...), score
  how positive or negative each post is, then ask: does the community
  mood move BEFORE search interest does?

SOURCES
  Reddit r/smiski via the official Reddit API - NOT YET RUN (needs
  login credentials). Currently SIMULATED: 3,600 template posts that
  use the real figure and series names.

HOW IT WAS RUN
  - Topics: BERTopic (it groups similar posts). I used a local text
    method (TF-IDF) so it works without downloading an AI model.
    One switch in the notebook upgrades it to sentence embeddings.
  - Sentiment: VADER, a standard word-based positive/negative scorer.
    Sentiment words were removed before topic-finding so topics
    reflect SUBJECT, while sentiment measures TONE separately.
  - Lead/lag: correlate sentiment this week with demand k weeks
    later; a peak at k > 0 means mood leads. Uncertainty comes from
    a "block bootstrap" because weekly data is autocorrelated.
  - Granger tests: a second check of "does A help predict B".

RESULTS (simulation - proves the method, not fan behaviour)
  - Topics found: Favourite figures 21% (very positive), Resale &
    prices 18% (negative), Display & glow 18% (positive), Scarcity &
    restocks 17% (negative), Pull rates & luck 13%, New releases ~13%.
  - Mood vs demand: strongest link about 2 weeks ahead (r about 0.15,
    weak). The simulator was built with a 2-week lead, so recovering
    it shows the method works. Statistical tests after correction
    were not significant.

BUSINESS TAKEAWAY
  If real data confirms it: scarcity and resale are the pain points
  to fix (restocks, purchase limits); display and "I pulled my
  favourite" posts are the shareable good news. Do not use tone as an
  early-warning signal unless the lead is stable.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
6. HOW I KEPT THE RESULTS HONEST
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  - ALWAYS A BASELINE: every model is shown next to a simple one and
    "how much better" with an uncertainty range.
  - NO CHEATING IN TESTS: whole-series hold-out, and a final period
    the model never saw.
  - UNCERTAINTY, NOT JUST ONE NUMBER: ranges on prices and forecasts,
    bootstrap bands on importance and correlation.
  - SMALL DATA WARNING: only 18 series, so simple models are fine.
  - LABELS EVERYWHERE: real vs simulated is stated on the website,
    in every notebook, and in data/processed/DATA_SOURCE.json.
  - Honest "negative" results are reported (nothing beat naive; the
    interval ranges under-cover; boosting barely beat the baseline).

7. THE WEBSITE  (site/index.html - just double-click it)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  Virtual blind box
    1. Pick one of 18 real series.  2. Pick one of 6 sealed boxes.
    3. Open it -> a random figure appears, weighted by the (assumed)
       pull odds, with its official description and a model-estimated
       resale value + range. Your collection fills in with a tracker
       (boxes opened, figures found, money spent vs estimated value).
  Findings sections
    Model vs baselines chart, SHAP chart, forecast chart with its
    uncertainty band and changepoints, topic chart, mood-vs-demand
    chart. Each has a "show data table" button and a takeaway box.
  Dark mode ("Lights off") makes the glow effect show.
  IMAGES: the real product photos are loaded from a local folder
  (site/img/figures) that is excluded from version control, because
  the artwork belongs to Dreams Inc. If the folder is missing the site
  shows my own placeholder sprites. Secret figures always use the
  sprite (the official site only shows a "?" graphic for them).
  The "predicted value" in the blind box uses SIMULATED prices, so it
  is a demo of the method, not a price quote.

10. QUESTIONS YOU MIGHT GET (and honest answers)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Q: Is the resale data real?
A: No. It is simulated on top of the real figure list. I need a real
   source (like eBay sold listings) to replace it.

Q: Then why trust the premium model?
A: Trust the method (honest validation, baselines, SHAP), not the
   numbers. Swap in real prices and the same notebook re-runs.

Q: Why did the fancy models not win?
A: Little data (18 series) and a trend that peaked and fell. Simple
   baselines are hard to beat then. That is a finding, not a failure.

Q: Where did the figures and dates come from?
A: smiski.com product and news pages (scraped politely). Older
   release dates are approximate and flagged.

Q: Are the images yours?
A: No, they are the maker's. They are used locally only and kept out
   of the shared code. The shareable version uses my own sprites.

Q: What does the changepoint finding mean?
A: Big jumps in search interest sit close to launches (Exercising,
   Moving, Hippers). That suggests launches matter, but it is not
   proof they caused the jumps.

11. GLOSSARY
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Baseline ........ a simple guess a model must beat to be worth using.
Blind box ....... sealed box; you do not know which figure is inside.
Changepoint ..... a moment when a trend abruptly shifts level.
Forecast ........ a prediction of future values.
Hold-out ........ data kept hidden from the model to test it fairly.
Leakage ......... accidentally letting test data hint at the answer.
MAE ............. average size of the model's mistakes.
Premium ......... resale price above retail, in percent.
SHAP ............ shows which inputs pushed a prediction up or down.
Sentiment ....... how positive or negative a piece of text is.
Simulated ....... generated by my code, not collected from the world.
Topic model ..... groups text by theme automatically (BERTopic).
Unofficial ...... fan project; not affiliated with Dreams Inc.
